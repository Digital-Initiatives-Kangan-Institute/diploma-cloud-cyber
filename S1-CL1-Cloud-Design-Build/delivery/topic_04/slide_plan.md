### C1 — Recommendation
- Teaches: [ICTICT517 PC 2.3] · [ICTICT517 PE 4]
- Kicker: from analysis to advice
- [BESPOKE] From analysis to advice
  - Evaluation tells you what the options are worth; a recommendation says what to do.
  - This is the turn from analyst to advisor — you stop weighing and start advising.
  - The board wants a clear call, backed by the evidence you've already built.
  image: none
  notes:
    Section 1 opener — the mindset shift from weighing options to advising a decision. Short, but it
    sets the tone for the whole section.
    • The turn (accent-bold): evaluation tells you what the options are WORTH; a recommendation says
    what to DO. You stop being the analyst and become the advisor.
    • Reassure the timid student: the board WANTS a clear call — sitting on the fence and listing
    options back to them is a non-answer.
    • The call isn't a leap — it's backed by the CBA and risk work from Topic 3; you're standing on
    evidence you've already built.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "a balanced consultant presents all options equally and lets the board
    choose." No — presenting options is Topic 3; the board is paying for your recommendation.
    Question to pose: "What's the difference between a report that says 'here are three options' and
    one that says 'do this'?" (the second is advice — and worth more).
    UoC/AT1 tie: opens PC 2.3 / PE 4 (prioritise + recommend) → AT1 §8 Recommendation.
- [BESPOKE] Prioritise first
  - Before recommending, rank the proposed changes (from your gap analysis): what matters most, what's urgent, what can wait.
  - A simple lens: impact (against the objectives) versus effort / urgency.
  - Prioritisation makes the plan sequenceable — and shows you're not boiling the ocean.
  image: none
  notes:
    Teach prioritisation as the step BEFORE the recommendation — you can't recommend or sequence work
    you haven't ranked. High-leverage; don't skip past it.
    • The changes come from their gap analysis (Topic 2/3): rank what matters most, what's urgent,
    what can wait.
    • Give them the lens: impact (against the objectives) versus effort / urgency — a simple 2-axis
    sort, not a weighted scoring model.
    • The payoff (accent-bold): prioritisation makes the plan SEQUENCEABLE later, and it shows the
    board you're not "boiling the ocean" — doing everything at once.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "prioritise = do the cheapest / easiest first." No — high-impact work
    can be worth doing first even if it's harder; impact leads, effort is the second axis.
    Question to pose: "If YAT can only fund half of the proposed changes this year, which do you do
    first — and on what basis?" (impact against the objectives, then urgency).
    UoC/AT1 tie: PC 2.3 (prioritise proposed changes to assist scheduling) + PE 4 → AT1 §8.
- [BESPOKE] Make the call
  - State the recommended option plainly — no hedging.
  - Justify it straight from the evidence:
    - the CBA result (cost over the horizon)
    - the risk / impact comparison
    - alignment with the strategic objectives
  - Name the trade-off you're accepting — a recommendation with no downside isn't trusted.
  image: none
  notes:
    The heart of the section — teach how to STATE and JUSTIFY the recommendation. Model the "no
    hedging" tone yourself as you talk.
    • State the recommended option plainly — one option, no weasel words ("we could possibly consider").
    • Justify it straight from the evidence they already have: the CBA result (cost over the horizon),
    the risk / impact comparison, and alignment with the strategic objectives (§2).
    • The line that earns trust (accent-bold): NAME the trade-off you're accepting — a recommendation
    with no downside reads as naive and the board won't believe it.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "justifying means re-arguing the whole CBA." No — POINT at the evidence
    section; the recommendation cites, it doesn't repeat.
    Question to pose: "You recommend moving Ledgerline to the cloud — what's the honest trade-off
    you're asking the board to accept?" (e.g. ongoing opex vs owned capex, migration risk).
    UoC/AT1 tie: PC 2.3 / PE 4 → AT1 §8 — the recommended option with evidence-linked justification.
- [BESPOKE] What a strong recommendation looks like
  - Decisive — one clear recommended option.
  - Evidence-linked — every reason traces to your CBA / risk / strategy work.
  - Honest — names the trade-off and why it's acceptable.
  - Actionable — leads naturally into the plan.
  image: none
  notes:
    A checklist slide — the four marks of a recommendation that lands. Use it as the quality bar they
    self-check the activity against.
    • Decisive — one clear recommended option, not a shortlist.
    • Evidence-linked — every reason traces back to their CBA / risk / strategy work (nothing new
    invented here).
    • Honest — names the trade-off and why it's acceptable.
    • Actionable (accent-bold) — it leads naturally into the plan; a recommendation you can't act on
    isn't finished.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "more reasons = stronger." No — a few reasons that each trace to real
    evidence beat a long list of assertions; padding weakens it.
    Question to pose: "Which of these four is the one students most often miss?" (honest — naming the
    trade-off; get them to commit to including one).
    UoC/AT1 tie: this is the quality bar for AT1 §8; a recommendation hitting all four is what the
    marking grid rewards.

  - ~½ page.
  - ⚠  Recommend, don't re-argue — point at the evidence you already built.
  timer: ~20 min, then we discuss
  image: none
  notes:
    Facilitation — first build task of the Topic. They add §8 to the SAME practice business case they
    started in Topic 2. Circulate; this is where analyst-to-advisor becomes real.
    Tell students: "In your working copy of the Business Case, add the Recommendation section for the
    YAT Accounting System (Ledgerline). Recommend, don't re-argue — point at the evidence you already
    built in Topic 3."
    Steps (put on the board):
    1. Prioritise your proposed changes (from your gap analysis) — impact vs effort/urgency.
    2. State the recommended option plainly — one option.
    3. Justify it from your CBA + risk + strategic alignment — CITE your own earlier sections.
    4. Name the trade-off you're accepting.
    Must produce: a ~½-page Recommendation section added to their practice BC — a prioritised call
    with evidence-linked justification and a named trade-off.
    Timing: ~20 min then discuss. Where they get stuck: they re-argue the whole CBA instead of citing
    it, or they hedge with two options — push them to pick ONE and point back at their evidence.
    Share-back prompt: take two recommendations and ask the room which trade-off is more honest.
    No-leakage note: this is PRACTICE on Ledgerline; AT1 assesses the same §8 skill on the LMS —

- [TAKEAWAYS] Section 1 · Recommendation
  - Prioritise the changes, then recommend — impact vs effort/urgency.
  - One clear option; justify it straight from the evidence; name the trade-off.
  - The recommendation is the hinge between the evidence and the plan.

### C2 — Action plan
- Teaches: [ICTICT517 PC 3.1] · [ICTICT517 PC 3.2] · [ICTICT517 PE 6] · [ICTICT517 KE 1]
- Kicker: how and when
- [BESPOKE] What the action plan is for
  - A recommendation says what and why; the action plan says how and when.
  - It turns a “yes” into execution the organisation can actually run.
  - And it proves the recommendation is feasible.
  image: none
  notes:
    Section 2 opener — the purpose of the action plan before its parts. Keep it to the "why" so the
    parts slide next has a job to do.
    • The distinction (walk it slowly): a recommendation says WHAT and WHY; the action plan says HOW
    and WHEN. Two different questions.
    • It turns a board's "yes" into execution the organisation can actually run — a decision no one can
    act on is worthless.
    • And (accent-bold) it PROVES the recommendation is feasible — a plan that holds together is
    evidence the call was realistic.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "the plan is where I design the whole solution." No — at AT1 level it's
    a board-ready roadmap (phases, milestones, metrics), not a full project plan with a Gantt and
    resourcing.
    Question to pose: "The board says yes to moving Ledgerline — what do they now need from you before
    anyone lifts a finger?" (how it happens and when — the plan).
    UoC/AT1 tie: opens PC 3.1 / 3.2, PE 6, KE 1 → AT1 §9 Action Plan.
- [BESPOKE] The parts of an action plan
  - Prioritised changes — what gets done, in priority order
  - Implementation schedule — phases / sequence / timeframe
  - Standards, targets & success metrics — how you'll know it worked
  - Implementation methods — how each change is delivered
  - Alignment with the change-management procedure — routing through governance
  kicker: five parts (becomes the Action Plan section)
  image: none
  notes:
    The structural spine of Section 2 — the five parts that BECOME the AT1 §9 Action Plan section.
    Walk each briefly; the following slides go deeper on the hard three.
    • 1. Prioritised changes — what gets done, in priority order (carries straight from Section 1).
    • 2. Implementation schedule — phases / sequence / timeframe.
    • 3. Standards, targets & success metrics — how you'll know it worked.
    • 4. Implementation methods — how each change is actually delivered.
    • 5. Alignment with the change-management procedure — routing through governance.
    Tell them this list IS the section skeleton — five headings they'll fill in the activity.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "a schedule alone is an action plan." No — a plan with dates but no
    standards, methods or governance is a wish-list; all five parts are needed.
    Question to pose: "Which of these five do students most often forget?" (standards/targets/metrics,
    and change-management alignment — the two that make it accountable and real).
    UoC/AT1 tie: KE 1 (key sections of an action plan) + PC 3.1 / 3.2, PE 6 → AT1 §9.1–§9.5.
- [BESPOKE] Sequencing & the schedule
  - Order the work by priority and dependency (you can't migrate data before the target exists).
  - Group into phases with a realistic timeframe.
  - Board-level — phases and milestones, not a day-by-day Gantt. Show the critical path.
  image: none
  notes:
    Teach how to turn the prioritised list into a phased schedule — the "when". Lean on a concrete
    dependency so it lands.
    • Order the work by priority AND dependency — make it tangible: you can't migrate data before the
    target environment exists. Dependencies constrain the order, not just priority.
    • Group the work into phases with a realistic timeframe — not everything in week one.
    • The level (accent-bold): BOARD-level — phases and milestones, show the critical path; NOT a
    day-by-day Gantt. Right altitude is the skill here.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "more detail is more professional." At board level the opposite is true
    — a day-by-day task list buries the decision; phases and milestones communicate.
    Question to pose: "What in a Ledgerline migration simply CAN'T happen until something else is done
    first?" (build the target before cutover; test before go-live) — that's a dependency.
    UoC/AT1 tie: PC 3.1 (prioritised schedule consistent with policy/procedures) → AT1 §9.
- [BESPOKE] Standards, targets & success metrics
  - Hold the work to the industry standards (Well-Architected, ISO 27017, Essential Eight, ITIL).
  - Set measurable targets: availability, recovery, cutover with no data loss, budget adherence.
  - Targets make the plan accountable — they define “done well”.
  image: none
  notes:
    Teach the part that makes the plan ACCOUNTABLE — the standards it's held to and the measurable
    targets that define "done well". Callback to Topic 1's standards here.
    • Hold the work to industry standards — Well-Architected, ISO 27017, Essential Eight, ITIL (the
    same rulebooks they met in Topic 1). The plan doesn't invent quality; it references a standard.
    • Set MEASURABLE targets: availability, recovery, cutover with no data loss, budget adherence —
    numbers, not adjectives.
    • The point (accent-bold): targets define "done well" — without them, no one can say whether the
    implementation succeeded.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "success metrics = 'the system works'." No — that's not measurable;
    a target is "99.9% availability" or "cutover with zero data loss", something you can check against.
    Question to pose: "How would YAT KNOW the Ledgerline migration succeeded — name one number you'd
    commit to?" (availability %, recovery time, zero-data-loss cutover, on-budget).
    UoC/AT1 tie: PC 3.2 (standards, targets, implementation methods) → AT1 §9.
- [BESPOKE] Align to the change-management procedure
  - Real change runs through governance.
  - Map the high-risk steps (e.g. cutover) to the change-management procedure:
    - change request → risk assessment → approval → change window
  - This is what separates a consultant's plan from a wish-list.
  image: none
  notes:
    Teach the part that separates a consultant's plan from a wish-list — routing high-risk steps
    through governance. This is often the missing piece in student plans.
    • Real change runs through governance; you don't just "do" a cutover — you route it.
    • Map the high-risk steps (e.g. cutover) to the change-management procedure: change request → risk
    assessment → approval → change window. Say those stages aloud.
    • The line (accent-bold): this alignment is what makes the plan credible to a board that runs on
    process — it shows you understand how organisations actually change things.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "change management is bureaucracy I can skip in a plan." No — for
    high-risk steps it's exactly what a board expects to see; skipping it reads as naive.
    Question to pose: "Cutover to the new Ledgerline is high-risk — what governance does it pass
    through before it happens?" (change request → risk assessment → approval → scheduled window).
    UoC/AT1 tie: PC 3.1 (consistency with organisational policy and procedures) → AT1 §9.
- [EX] Build the Action Plan
  - In your working copy of the Business Case, add the Action Plan for the Accounting System.
  - Include all five parts:
    - prioritised changes · implementation schedule (phases)
    - standards, targets & success metrics · implementation methods
    - alignment with the change-management procedure
  - ~1 page.
  - ⚠  Board-level — phases, milestones and metrics, not a day-by-day task list.
  timer: ~30 min, then we compare
  image: none
  notes:
    Facilitation — the biggest build task of the Topic. They add §9 to their practice BC with all
    five parts. Circulate and watch the altitude (board-level, not task-level).
    Tell students: "In your working copy of the Business Case, add the Action Plan for the YAT
    Accounting System. Board-level — phases, milestones and metrics, not a day-by-day task list."
    Steps (put on the board) — include all five parts:
    1. Prioritised changes.
    2. Implementation schedule (phases, sequence, timeframe).
    3. Standards, targets & success metrics.
    4. Implementation methods (how each change is delivered).
    5. Alignment with the change-management procedure.
    Must produce: a ~1-page Action Plan section covering all five parts — a phased schedule with
    measurable targets and high-risk steps routed through governance.
    Timing: ~30 min then compare. Where they get stuck: they drop into a day-by-day task list (pull
    them back up to phases/milestones), or they forget targets and change-management — the two parts
    most often missed. Have the five-part list on the board the whole time.
    Share-back prompt: compare two schedules — which one handles dependencies and the critical path?
    No-leakage note: PRACTICE on Ledgerline; AT1 assesses the same §9 plan on the LMS — comparable,
    not identical.
    UoC/AT1 tie: PC 3.1 / 3.2, PE 6, KE 1 → AT1 Part A §9.
- [TAKEAWAYS] Section 2 · Action plan
  - The plan turns the recommendation into executable phases.
  - Five parts: prioritised changes · schedule · standards/targets/metrics · methods · change-mgmt alignment.
  - Board-level detail; route high-risk steps through governance; make it measurable.

### C3 — The decision requested
- Teaches: [ICTICT517 PC 3.3]
- Kicker: make the “yes” easy
- [BESPOKE] Close with a clear ask
  - A business case that doesn't ask for anything gets “noted”, not approved.
  - The final section makes the decision easy: state exactly what you want approved, today.
  image: none
  notes:
    Section 3 opener — why the case must end with a specific ask. Short and sharp; it sets up the
    "what to ask for" slide.
    • The failure mode: a business case that doesn't ASK for anything gets "noted", not approved — it
    dies politely in the minutes.
    • The fix (accent-bold): the final section makes the decision easy — state exactly what you want
    approved, today. Make saying "yes" a single word.
    Misconception to pre-empt: "the recommendation already IS the ask." Not quite — the recommendation
    is your advice; the ask is the specific decision(s) you want the board to make now (option + plan +
    budget). Spell it out separately.
    Question to pose: "You've recommended and planned — what's the one sentence that gets the board to
    actually decide today?" (a specific, answerable ask).
    UoC/AT1 tie: opens PC 3.3 (provide action plan for feedback/approval — the written ask) → AT1 §10.
- [BESPOKE] What to ask for
  - Approve the recommended option.
  - Approve the action plan as the roadmap.
  - Authorise the budget envelope (the figure from your CBA).
  - List the next steps; defer the high-risk gates to their change-management approval.
  - The ask should be answerable with a single “approved”.
  kicker: be specific
  image: none
  notes:
    Teach the CONTENT of the ask — be specific enough that the board can answer with one word. This is
    the slide that makes §10 concrete.
    • Approve the recommended option.
    • Approve the action plan as the roadmap.
    • Authorise the budget envelope — the actual figure from their CBA (not "some funding").
    • List the next steps; defer the high-risk gates to their change-management approval (you're not
    asking the board to approve the cutover itself today, just the plan and budget).
    • The test (accent-bold): the ask should be answerable with a single "approved".
    Misconception to pre-empt: "asking for the budget means asking approval for every step." No —
    authorise the envelope and the plan; the high-risk gates get their OWN approval later via change
    management. Don't over-ask.
    Question to pose: "Name the three things you're asking YAT's board to approve." (the option, the
    plan, the budget figure).
    UoC/AT1 tie: PC 3.3 → AT1 §10 Next Steps and Decision Requested.
